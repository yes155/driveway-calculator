## How much is it to replace a driveway?

**Angi lists asphalt driveway replacement at $8–$15 per square foot including removal; other surfaces require their own installation and removal scope.**

For concrete, the researched plain-installation benchmark is $6–$10 per square foot and the selected demolition range is $2–$6 per square foot. The calculator adds these components when demolition is required; a combined figure is identified as a calculation, not a separately observed national quote. No hand-entered example total replaces the calculator output.

## How much does it cost to replace a driveway with another surface?

**Switching materials changes demolition, subbase work and the new surface specification.**

Selected installed benchmarks are $4–$10 per square foot for a new gravel driveway and $10–$30 for a generic paver driveway. Those rates do not establish the cost of every material change. An old asphalt surface, reinforced concrete slab and failed paver base have different removal and disposal requirements.

## What belongs in demolition and hauling?

**The quote describes breaking up the old surface, loading, hauling and disposal.**

Identify the pavement material, thickness, reinforcement and access restrictions. Ask whether disposal fees and protection of nearby utilities are included. Do not add demolition again to an asphalt replacement quote that already covers it. Separately priced drainage corrections or substantial excavation need an explicit scope.

## How does subbase condition change driveway replacement cost?

**Rebuilding failed support is separate from merely changing the wearing surface.**

The installer checks support, compaction, drainage and required finished elevation. An asphalt overlay on a sound existing driveway differs from replacement after removal. Use the repaving and concrete replacement guides to compare those operations, then enter the measured sections in the surface comparison calculator. Obtain a local written quote for the work the national benchmark does not specify. An estimate identifies assumptions; it does not promise the contractor’s final price.
